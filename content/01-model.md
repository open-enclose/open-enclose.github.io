# The Model

This page walks through how the paper's results are derived — the steps, the substitutions
that make the algebra collapse, and the intuition for why each threshold looks the way it
does. It follows the paper's order, and every derivation ends at a numbered equation you
can find there.

It is deliberately a *guide*, not a proof. The complete formal derivations, equation by
equation, are in the [Mathematical Appendix](04-derivations.md). Every curve shown here is
generated by the [`enclose`](https://github.com/open-enclose/open-enclose.github.io)
package, which is checked against symbolic derivations in `enclose/symbolic.py` — so the
figures and the algebra cannot silently drift apart.

:::{note} Notation
Land and labor endowments are $\bar T$ and $\bar L$, with **population density**
$\bar l = \bar L / \bar T$. Enclosed shares are $t_e = T_e/\bar T$ and $l_e = L_e/\bar L$.
$\theta$ is the relative TFP of enclosed land, $\alpha$ the labor share, $c$ the per-unit
enclosure cost and $A$ total factor productivity.

Because every locus below equates a marginal benefit (which scales with $A$) to a marginal
cost (which scales with $c$), $A$ and $c$ only ever appear as the ratio $c/A$. All diagrams
are drawn for $c/A = 1$ and $\alpha = 2/3$.
:::

---

## 1. Technology and the planner's problem

Both sectors use Cobb-Douglas technology; enclosure raises TFP by the factor $\theta$. The
planner chooses how much land to enclose, $t_e$, and how to split labor, $l_e$, to maximise
output per unit land net of enclosure costs — equations (1)–(2).

The first thing to notice is that the whole problem collapses onto a single object. Setting
$MP_L^e = MP_L^c$ and solving gives the **labor reaction function**, equation (4):

$$
l_e^1(t_e) = \frac{\Lambda_o\, t_e}{1+(\Lambda_o-1)t_e},
\qquad \Lambda_o = \theta^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
$$

Everything downstream is a function of $\Lambda$. That is what makes closed forms possible
at all, and it is worth pausing on what $\Lambda$ *means*: it is the factor by which the
enclosed sector's labor intensity exceeds the economy's average.

```{figure} ../Figures/labor_reaction.png
:name: fig-labor-reaction
:width: 75%

The labor reaction function $l_e(t_e)$. The dotted 45° line is $l_e = t_e$ — labor and land
enclosed in equal proportion. The planner's curve ($\mu=1$) bows *above* it: enclosed land
is worked more intensively. Under open access ($\mu=0$) the curve bows *below*. The vertical
gap between them at any $t_e$ is the labor misallocation that Section 5 is about.
```

Substituting (4) back into the objective gives output per unit land, equation (5):

$$
z_1(t_e) = A\,\bar l^{\alpha}\left[1+(\Lambda_o-1)t_e\right]^{1-\alpha}
$$

This is the step that does the work. What looked like a two-variable optimisation is now a
one-dimensional function of $t_e$, and a concave one.

## 2. When should land be enclosed?

Because $z_1$ is concave, the planner's problem is solved by comparing its slope to the
enclosure cost $c$. Differentiating and evaluating at the two endpoints gives the parameter
thresholds directly.

At $t_e=0$, setting $z_1'(0) = c$ and solving for density gives equation (6):

$$
\bar l_0^1 = \left[\frac{c/A}{(1-\alpha)(\Lambda_o-1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}
$$

and at $t_e=1$, equation (7) — which turns out to be the same locus scaled by $\Lambda_o$:

$$
\bar l_1^1 = \Lambda_o \cdot \bar l_0^1
$$

Below $\bar l_0^1$ no enclosure is optimal; above $\bar l_1^1$ full enclosure is; between
them, partial. Note the shape of these expressions: every locus is
$\bar l = (c/A)^{1/\alpha} \cdot g(\theta)$, so a change in costs or productivity shifts
*all* loci vertically by the same amount. That is why the diagrams can be drawn once at
$c/A=1$ without loss.

```{figure} ../Figures/social_opt_cond.png
:name: fig-social
:width: 90%

Socially efficient enclosure, in $(\theta,\ \ln\bar l)$ space. The shaded band between
$\bar l_0^1$ and $\bar l_1^1$ is where partial enclosure is optimal.
```

The economics is Boserupian: at low density, enclosure costs exceed the gains and the
commons persists. Population growth pushes the economy up through $\bar l_0^1$, and
enclosure begins.

## 3. Decentralized enclosure

Now replace the planner with private enclosers. The key change is on the commons: labor
enters until the *average* product equals the wage, not the marginal product — the classic
open-access distortion, equations (8)–(9).

That single substitution changes $\Lambda$ and nothing else. The reaction function keeps
its form, equation (10), but with

$$
\Lambda = (\alpha\theta)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
$$

in place of $\Lambda_o = \theta^{1/(1-\alpha)}$. The $\alpha$ inside is the whole
distortion.

This immediately explains the threshold that organises the rest of the paper. $\Lambda = 1$
when $\alpha\theta = 1$, i.e. at

$$
\theta_H = \frac{1}{\alpha}
$$

Below $\theta_H$, $\Lambda<1$ and private enclosure is labor-*extensive* — enclosing land
pushes labor onto the commons. Above it, enclosure is labor-intensive, as the planner's
would be. Almost every qualitative claim in the paper turns on which side of $\theta_H$ the
economy sits.

The private return to enclosing a marginal parcel, equation (12), is

$$
r(t_e) = (1-\alpha)\,\theta\,\Lambda^{\alpha} A\,\bar l^{\alpha}
\left[1+(\Lambda-1)t_e\right]^{-\alpha}
$$

Setting $r(0)=c$ and $r(1)=c$ gives the decentralized thresholds, equations (13)–(14),
exactly as before.

### Strategic complements, and why that needs global games

Notice the exponent $-\alpha$ on the bracket. When $\Lambda<1$ the bracket *decreases* in
$t_e$, so $r$ *increases* — enclosure by others raises my return to enclosing. Decisions are
strategic complements, and both "nobody encloses" and "everybody encloses" are equilibria.
The model has nothing to say about which occurs.

The global-games refinement resolves this. Under Laplacian beliefs an encloser evaluates the
expected payoff over $t_e$ uniform on $[0,1]$, so the selection condition integrates the
return rather than evaluating it at a point:

$$
\int_0^1 \left[r(t_e) - c\right]\,dt_e = 0
$$

Carrying out the integral and solving for density gives the risk-dominance locus,
equation (15) — the dashed red line $\bar l_{gg}^d$ in the figures below. Above it, the
enclosure cascade tips.

```{figure} ../Figures/nash_so_comp.png
:name: fig-nash
:width: 90%

Decentralized enclosure regions (red) against the first-best band (black). The
"multiplicity" region left of $\theta_H$ is where enclosure decisions are strategic
complements; $\bar l_{gg}^d$ selects between the corner equilibria there.
```

## 4. Is decentralized enclosure second-best?

The comparison that matters is not planner vs. private — the planner also reallocates labor
efficiently, which no decentralized process does. The right benchmark is a *constrained*
planner who chooses $t_e$ but must live with the decentralized labor allocation. That gives
$z_0(t_e)$, equation (17).

$z_0$ behaves differently from $z_1$ in one important way: below $\theta_H$ it is **convex**
in $t_e$. So the optimum is at a corner, and finding it means comparing *endpoint values*
rather than solving a first-order condition:

$$
z_0(1) = z_0(0)
$$

Solving that for density gives $\bar l^s$, equation (18) — a locus obtained by a genuinely
different route from all the others. Above $\theta_H$, where $z_0$ is concave again, the
usual first-order conditions give equations (19)–(20).

```{figure} ../Figures/comparison.png
:name: fig-compare
:width: 90%

Decentralized outcomes against the second-best. Red hatching (left of $\theta_H$): full
enclosure occurs though no enclosure would yield higher net output. Blue hatching (right):
too little enclosure, the finer band being a coordination failure.
```

Two distinct inefficiencies appear, and they point in opposite directions — which is why the
paper resists a simple "enclosure is excessive" reading.

## 5. Where the wedge comes from

Decomposing the constrained planner's marginal benefit, equation (21), isolates two things
private enclosers ignore.

**Displaced rents.** The commons is not idle; it yields rents captured by existing users
through possession. Enclosers appropriate these without paying for them, so they enclose
whenever $\theta F_T^e A\bar f > c$, even when the *net* social gain
$(\theta F_T^e - F_T^c)A\bar f$ falls short. This drives **excessive** enclosure.

**The labor reallocation gain.** On the commons, labor enters until average product equals
the wage, pushing $MP_L^c$ below $\theta MP_L^e$. Enclosure releases workers from that
congestion — but enclosers pay the full market wage regardless, so they capture none of the
efficiency dividend. This drives **insufficient** enclosure.

```{figure} ../Figures/labor_misallocation.png
:name: fig-misallocation
:width: 85%

The labor wedge. Commons labor enters until $AP_L^c$ — not $MP_L^c$ — equals the wage, so
$MP_L^c$ sits below $MP_L^e$. The hatched area between $l_e^*$ and $l_e^o$ is the efficiency
loss enclosure could recover but enclosers are not paid for.
```

## 6. The extended model: governance and compensation

The benchmark assumes an unregulated commons and uncompensated enclosure. Both are extreme.
Sections 5.1–5.3 relax them with two parameters, each targeting one of the wedges above.

### $\mu$ — the commons regulates itself

Let $\mu \in [0,1]$ measure how well the community restrains entry onto the commons.
Redoing the labor-allocation condition with that restraint gives equation (23) — and,
satisfyingly, it changes nothing except $\Lambda$ once more:

$$
\Lambda_\mu = \left(\frac{\alpha\theta}{1-\mu(1-\alpha)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}
$$

At $\mu=0$ this is the open-access $\Lambda$; at $\mu=1$ it is the planner's $\Lambda_o$.
The single parameter interpolates between the two regimes, which is why the same closed
forms carry through the whole extended model.

Since $\Lambda_\mu=1$ pins the complements/substitutes threshold, governance moves it —
equation (24):

$$
\theta_H^\mu = \frac{1}{\alpha} - \mu\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}
$$

falling from $1/\alpha$ at $\mu=0$ to $1$ at $\mu=1$. Better governance shrinks the region
of multiplicity and dissipative races.

### $\tau$ — enclosers must compensate

Let $\tau \in [0,1]$ be the share of displaced rents an encloser must pay. The enclosure
condition becomes $r^e_\mu - \tau r^c_\mu \geq c$, and along the reaction function this is

$$
r^e_\mu(t_e) - \tau\,r^c_\mu(t_e)
= (1-\alpha)A\bar l^{\alpha}\left(\theta\Lambda_\mu^{\alpha} - \tau\right)
\left[1+(\Lambda_\mu-1)t_e\right]^{-\alpha}
$$

Compare this with equation (12): the only change is $\theta\Lambda_\mu^\alpha$ becoming
$\theta\Lambda_\mu^\alpha - \tau$. Every threshold that followed from (12) therefore
survives with that one substitution — equations (26)–(27). Integrating this expression over
$t_e\in[0,1]$ rather than evaluating it at an endpoint gives the $\tau$-extended
global-games locus in the same way as before.

At $\tau=1$ the locus diverges at $\theta = \alpha^{-\alpha}$: with full compensation, no
population density makes a raid profitable below that productivity gain.

### Both together

The two parameters close the two wedges of Section 5, and they close them *jointly*.
Setting $\mu=1$ makes $\Lambda_\mu = \Lambda_o$; then the identity
$\theta\Lambda_o^{\alpha} = \Lambda_o$ collapses the decentralized denominator into the
planner's, and the decentralized loci become **identical** to the first-best ones — not
merely close. Neither parameter alone suffices.

```{figure} ../Figures/new_comp_fig4x4.png
:name: fig-4x4
:width: 100%

Governance and compensation. (a) the benchmark; (b) $\mu=1$ alone; (c) $\tau=1$ alone;
(d) both — where the decentralized loci (dashed red) lie exactly on the planner's (black).

```
